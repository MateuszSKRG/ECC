## 1. Lockheed Martin Skunk Works: organization, Kelly Johnson's 14 rules, and how the A-12/SR-71, F-117, YF-22/F-22 and X-35/F-35 were developed

### Takeaway
Skunk Works delivered black programs fast. Small, autonomous teams under Kelly's 14 rules took Have Blue from contract to first flight in about 20 months and the F-117 from go-ahead to first flight in 31 months (IOC at 60 months). Large competitive programs were much slower: the ATF demonstration/validation (Dem/Val) phase took about 47 months from team selection to the YF-22's first flight, and the F-22 logged nearly 44,000 wind-tunnel hours before it first flew. Each program's main technical risk was a new capability: titanium manufacture and the J58 engine on OXCART, signature control on Have Blue/F-117, and weight growth and engine thrust on the ATF.

### Cited Findings

**Organization and Kelly's rules**
- Lockheed Martin says Kelly Johnson's "14 Rules and Practices" are still in use at Skunk Works today. The company ties them to small, empowered teams and streamlined processes. — [Lockheed Martin, Skunk Works origin story](https://lockheedmartin.com/en-us/who-we-are/business-areas/aeronautics/skunkworks/skunk-works-origin-story.html)
- The rules started on the XP-80 project in 1943 and were formalized in the early 1950s. Wording differs between published versions, but the ideas are the same. — [Libarynth, "The 14 Rules of Skunk Works"](https://lib.fo.am/skunkworks); [Lockheed Martin origin story](https://lockheedmartin.com/en-us/who-we-are/business-areas/aeronautics/skunkworks/skunk-works-origin-story.html)
- The 14 rules, paraphrased from the commonly published text. Search summaries confirmed rules 1, 3, 6 and 13 explicitly; I could not open the page to check the exact wording of the others. — [Libarynth](https://lib.fo.am/skunkworks)
  1. The Skunk Works manager has practically complete control of the program and reports to a division president or higher.
  2. Strong but small project offices on both the customer and contractor side.
  3. The number of people connected with the project is restricted "in an almost vicious manner", using 10–25% of the staff of a "normal" program.
  4. A very simple drawing and drawing-release system that makes changes easy.
  5. A minimum of reports, but important work is recorded thoroughly.
  6. A monthly cost review that includes cost projected to program completion. The books are not 90 days late, and the customer gets no surprise overruns.
  7. The contractor takes more than normal responsibility for getting good subcontract bids; commercial bid procedures are often better than military ones.
  8. Inspection is streamlined and pushed back to vendors, with no duplicated inspection.
  9. The contractor is delegated authority to flight-test its own product, or it loses its competence to design.
  10. Hardware specifications are agreed before contracting. A clause lists which military specs will knowingly not be met, and why.
  11. Funding arrives on time, so the contractor does not have to finance government work.
  12. Mutual trust and day-to-day liaison between customer and contractor, which cuts paperwork.
  13. Outside access to the project and its people is strictly controlled by security measures.
  14. Good performance is rewarded with pay that is not based on the number of people supervised.
- Ben Rich (1925–1995) was the second director of Skunk Works, serving 1975–1991, and led the stealth effort. Rich obtained a special Air Force exemption so that Bill Park could serve as chief test pilot. — [MiGFlug, Ben Rich profile](https://migflug.com/afterburner/ben-rich-skunk-works-father-of-stealth/)

**A-12 OXCART / SR-71 (1959–1968 data, historical)**
- The CIA selected Lockheed's design in 1959. Skunk Works built it under the OXCART code name. The CIA first awarded Lockheed a four-month contract for antiradar studies, aerodynamic structural tests and engineering designs. — [GlobalSecurity, A-12 OXCART](https://www.globalsecurity.org/intell/systems/a-12.htm)
- First flight was in April 1962 at Groom Lake. Sources give 25 April, or a 26 April shakedown flight by Lou Schalk followed by the first official flight on 30 April. The first airframe flew on interim engines because the Pratt & Whitney J58 was not ready. — [Skytamer, A-12](https://www.skytamer.com/Lockheed_1962_A-12.html); [Space Camp, A-12 Article 127](https://www.spacecamp.com/tour/ac/A12Oxcart); [GlobalSecurity](https://www.globalsecurity.org/intell/systems/a-12.htm)
- The airframe was "largely titanium", a material the US industry had little experience working at scale. The program effectively had to build its own industrial base. The team designed it without digital computers. — [GlobalSecurity](https://www.globalsecurity.org/intell/systems/a-12.htm); [19FortyFive (2022)](https://www.19fortyfive.com/2022/02/a-12-oxcart-the-cias-very-own-sr-71-mach-3-spy-plane/)
- One article claims the US bought titanium from the USSR through shell companies. Only one source in the results says this; it is uncorroborated. — [19FortyFive (Apr 2025)](https://www.19fortyfive.com/2025/04/a-12-oxcart-the-mach-3-cia-spy-plane-faster-than-sr-71-blackbird/)
- The CIA ordered 13 A-12s and lost 5 in accidents. Robert McNamara cancelled the program in 1968. — [National Security Journal](https://nationalsecurityjournal.org/the-cia-built-13-a-12-oxcarts-to-spy-on-russia-then-lost-5-of-them-in-accidents-before-the-program-was-killed-in-1968/)
- The A-12 was declared operational in November 1965 after validation flights reached Mach 3.29 at 90,000 ft. — [CIA, "The Oxcart Story"](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/The-Oxcart-Story.pdf), as summarized by search; the PDF was not opened directly.

**Have Blue / F-117 (1976–1983 data, historical)**
- In April 1976 the USAF Flight Dynamics Laboratory contracted Skunk Works for two 60%-scale flyable Have Blue demonstrators. — [Lockheed Have Blue (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_Have_Blue); [f-117a.com, Have Blue](http://www.f-117a.com/Have.html)
- Have Blue first flew on 1 December 1977 with Bill Park as pilot; Ken Dyson flew the second aircraft. First flight came about 20 months after contract award. — [Lockheed Have Blue (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_Have_Blue); [MiGFlug, Have Blue](https://migflug.com/afterburner/have-blue-the-ugly-prototype-that-invented-stealth/)
- The two Have Blue aircraft cost $37 million or $35 million in total, depending on the source. — [MiGFlug, Have Blue](https://migflug.com/afterburner/have-blue-the-ugly-prototype-that-invented-stealth/); [F-117 Stealth Fighter Association](https://www.f117sfa.org/f117-development)
- Production (Senior Trend) was decided on 1 November 1978, with Ben Rich leading and Alan Brown as project manager. The first YF-117A flew on 18 June 1981. — [F-117 Stealth Fighter Association](https://www.f117sfa.org/f117-development)
- The F-117 went from design go-ahead to first flight in 31 months and to IOC in 60 months. Skunk Works built 59 production F-117s at a unit flyaway cost of $42.6 million (then-year dollars). — [Air & Space Forces Magazine, "How the Skunk Works Fielded Stealth"](https://www.airandspaceforces.com/article/1192stealth/)

**YF-22 / F-22 (ATF, 1981–1991 for the competition)**
- ATF timeline: request for information (RFI) in 1981, request for proposals (RFP) in 1985. — [Advanced Tactical Fighter (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advanced_Tactical_Fighter)
- On 31 October 1986 the USAF picked two Dem/Val teams: Lockheed/Boeing/General Dynamics and Northrop/McDonnell Douglas. Dem/Val is described as a 54-month program in one source and 50 months in another. — [Lockheed YF-22 (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_YF-22); [Advanced Tactical Fighter (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advanced_Tactical_Fighter)
- The YF-23 first flew on 27 August 1990 and the YF-22 on 29 September 1990. Secretary of the Air Force Donald Rice announced the Lockheed team as winner on 23 April 1991. — [Northrop YF-23 (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northrop_YF-23); [Lockheed YF-22 (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_YF-22)
- Wind tunnel: 19,195 test hours in YF-22 Dem/Val plus 7,005 hours before Dem/Val. A secondary figure gives about 18,000 hours by the end of Dem/Val. — [GlobalSecurity, F-22 Other Testing](https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/systems/aircraft/f-22-test.htm)
- Lockheed says the F-22 reached nearly 44,000 wind-tunnel test hours by its first flight. Another source totals 43,889 hours across YF-22 and F-22 configurations. — [Lockheed Martin, F-22 Raptor History](https://www.lockheedmartin.com/en-us/news/features/history/f-22.html); [GlobalSecurity](https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/systems/aircraft/f-22-test.htm)
- Key problem, weight growth: the F119 thrust class was raised from 30,000 lbf to 35,000 lbf after the ATF's gross weight grew. — [Pratt & Whitney F119 (Wikipedia mirror)](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/F119); [GlobalSecurity, F-22 F119](https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/systems/aircraft/f-22-f119.htm)

**X-35 / F-35 (1996–2001 concept demonstration)**
- The Concept Demonstration Phase ran 1996–2001, Lockheed's X-35 against Boeing's X-32. — [Lockheed Martin X-35 (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_Martin_X-35)
- The X-35A first flew on 24 October 2000 from Air Force Plant 42, Palmdale. — [Lockheed Martin press release, 24 Oct 2000](https://news.lockheedmartin.com/2000-10-24-Lockheed-Martin-Joint-Strike-Fighter-Takes-Flight)
- The X-35C first flew on 16 December 2000; X-35B short take-off and vertical landing (STOVL) testing followed in 2001. — [GlobalSecurity, X-35 test](https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/systems/aircraft/x-35-test.htm); [Lockheed Martin X-35 (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_Martin_X-35)
- The System Development and Demonstration (SDD) contract went to Lockheed Martin, with Northrop Grumman and BAE Systems, on 26 October 2001. The program was then projected at more than $200 billion for about 3,000 aircraft; this was a projection, not a contract value. — [Lockheed Martin X-35 (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_Martin_X-35); [Lockheed Martin F-35 timeline](https://view.ceros.com/lockheed-martin/the-f35-history-timeline)

### Inferences
- Time to first flight, from the cited dates:
  - A-12: CIA selection (1959) to first flight (April 1962) took about 2.5–3 years. The exact contract date was not retrieved.
  - Have Blue: about 20 months.
  - F-117: 31 months from go-ahead.
  - YF-22: 47 months from Dem/Val team selection, about 9 years from the 1981 RFI and about 5 years from the 1985 RFP.
  - X-35A: about 4 years from the 1996 start of concept demonstration.
- The trend: secret single-customer programs under Kelly's rules ran roughly 2× faster than open competitive programs.
- Wind-tunnel effort grew by an order of magnitude from the slide-rule era to the ATF, with about 26,000 hours through Dem/Val and about 44,000 hours by F-22 first flight. A two-person digital study cannot reproduce this. It should plan CFD validation against published test data and accept high uncertainty in aerodynamic and signature predictions.
- OXCART's main risks were material and industrial: titanium at scale, and an engine that was late. The ATF's main risk was weight growth, which forced a 17% increase in engine thrust class. Both point to propulsion and materials maturity, not aerodynamics, as what decides the schedule for a high-speed design.

### Gaps
- Team sizes (engineers and shop staff) for the A-12, Have Blue, F-117, YF-22 and X-35 were not found. The XP-80 "143 days" story and its 1943 team size were not confirmed in the results.
- Wind-tunnel and RCS-range hours for the A-12/SR-71, Have Blue and the YF-23 were not found. A-12 RCS testing ("Archangel" models) was not covered in the results.
- The exact OXCART contract date and value (commonly said to be January 1960 for 12 aircraft) were not verified.
- The search results did not cover Have Blue's design method (faceting and the Echo-1 RCS prediction code).
- F-22 engineering and manufacturing development (EMD) milestones (August 1991 award, first EMD flight, IOC in December 2005) and F-35 first flight/IOC dates were not verified in this session.
- Wording of Kelly's rules 2, 4, 5 and 7–12 is a paraphrase of the standard text; it was not checked against the Lockheed Martin page.

## 2. Specification tables: F-22A, F-35A, SR-71A, A-12, YF-12A, F-117A, YF-23, F-15C

### Takeaway
Official fact sheets give only rounded, often dated values, and several key parameters were not retrievable: F-22 wing area and internal fuel, F-35A engine thrust and maximum takeoff weight (MTOW), F-15C weights. The Mach 3 Blackbirds were very large: 1,795–1,800 ft² of wing, MTOW 117,000–170,000 lb, and roughly 80,000 lb of fuel. Their sea-level thrust-to-weight (T/W) was only about 0.4–0.5. Stealth fighters sit at 460–900 ft² of wing area, with T/W around 1. Every RCS figure is unofficial.

  - Sea-level static T/W at MTOW = 2 × 35,000 / 83,500 = 0.84.
  - Empty/MTOW fraction = 43,340 / 83,500 = 0.52.
- **F-35A:**
  - Empty weight + full internal fuel = 29,300 + 18,250 = 47,550 lb, without pilot or weapons.
  - Wing loading at that weight = 103 lb/ft² (≈505 kg/m²).
  - Internal fuel fraction of (empty + fuel) = 0.38.
- **SR-71A:**
  - Wing loading at 140,000 lb gross = 78 lb/ft² (≈380 kg/m²).
  - Static T/W = 65,000 / 140,000 = 0.46, or 0.38 at 170,000 lb.
  - Fuel fraction = 80,000 / 140,000 ≈ 0.57.
  - Fuel/(empty + fuel) ≈ 0.54–0.58, depending on the empty-weight figure.
  - A Mach 3.2 cruiser is mostly a fuel tank, with about 4× the F-35A's wing area.
- **A-12:**
  - Wing loading ≈ 124,600 / 1,795 = 69 lb/ft².
  - Empty/MTOW ≈ 0.44.
  - T/W ≈ 0.52, assuming the same 32,500 lb J58 rating as the SR-71. That rating was not separately verified for the A-12.
- **F-117A:** T/W at max weight ≈ 21,200 / 52,500 = 0.40 using the 10,600 lb figure. It was subsonic, with no afterburner.
- **YF-23:**
  - T/W at about 63,000 lb estimated takeoff weight ≈ 70,000 / 63,000 = 1.11.
  - Wing loading ≈ 70 lb/ft².
  - Both values rest on estimated weights.
- **Takeaway for the design study:** a stealth Mach 3 fighter must combine fighter-class T/W (about 0.85–1.1) and wing loading (about 70–105 lb/ft²) with a Blackbird-class fuel fraction (≥0.5) if it is to cruise at Mach 3 rather than dash there. This is the central sizing conflict.
- **F-22 material shares:** the 39% vs 42% titanium difference most likely reflects total versus structural weight. Use 39–42% Ti and 24% composites as the range.

## 3. US technology state of the art: sensor fusion, AESA radars, EOTS/DAS, integrated core processor, thrust vectoring, LO maintenance, software size

### Takeaway
The US benchmark is: AESA radars with roughly 1,500–2,000 transmit/receive (T/R) modules (APG-77) and about 1,676 (APG-81); a six-aperture spherical infrared Distributed Aperture System (DAS); an internal Electro-Optical Targeting System (EOTS) combining forward-looking infrared (FLIR), infrared search and track (IRST) and laser; and sensor fusion on an integrated core processor (ICP) over a Fibre Channel backbone. The F-35 now runs on Technology Refresh 3 (TR-3), the computer backbone for the Block 4 upgrade. Software grew from about 1–1.4 million lines of code (F-22) to more than 8 million onboard (F-35), or about 24 million including offboard systems. The F119 offers ±20° pitch-only thrust vectoring at an engine thrust-to-weight above 9.

### Inferences
- Software is now the dominant development risk. Onboard code grew about 6–8× from the F-22 to the F-35, and total code to about 24 million lines. Any new design study should treat mission-system software architecture (open, modular, updatable, as the Block 4/TR-3 experience suggests) as a first-order item, not a back-end task.
- At Mach 3, aerodynamic heating would raise skin and aperture temperatures well above the regimes these sensors operate in. Apertures for the AESA, DAS and EOTS would need thermal design that has no published US fighter precedent. The SR-71 carried no comparable fused sensor suite.
- Thrust vectoring on the F119 is pitch-only and only ±20°. That is a modest, LO-compatible choice. 2D nozzles also help rear-aspect RCS and IR shielding.

## 5. Why the US moved away from Mach 3 fighters/interceptors, and why the ATF chose about Mach 1.5+ supercruise plus stealth; F-22 published max speed

### Takeaway
US Mach 3 interceptors were cancelled mainly for cost and strategy. The XF-108 was cancelled on 23 September 1959 as ICBMs replaced bombers as the main Soviet threat, with Soviet SAM developments also cited. The F-12B was terminated in late 1967 to early 1968 in cost cuts, as continental air defense lost priority. The ATF requirement evolved from "reduced RCS" to "low observability" plus supercruise. The tactical logic is that supercruise without afterburner avoids the afterburner plume's IR and radar signature, and engine technology, not aerodynamics, was the limiting factor. The F-22 is officially "Mach 2 class"; Mach 2.25 top speed and Mach 1.82 supercruise are commonly cited.

### Inferences
- These are physics-based inferences: standard isentropic stagnation temperature T0 = T∞(1 + 0.2M²), with T∞ = 216.65 K in the stratosphere.

  | Mach | Stagnation temperature | Note |
  |---|---|---|
  | 1.82 | ≈ 360 K (87 °C) | F-22 supercruise |
  | 2.25 | ≈ 436 K (163 °C) | F-22 top speed |
  | 3.0 | ≈ 607 K (333 °C) | Target |
  | 3.2 | ≈ 660 K (387 °C) | SR-71 cruise |

  With a turbulent recovery factor of about 0.89, the Mach 3 adiabatic wall temperature is about 564 K (≈290 °C).
  - Going from Mach 2.25 to Mach 3 roughly doubles the temperature rise above ambient, and nearly quadruples it from F-22 supercruise.
  - This plausibly explains why the A-12/SR-71 needed a mostly titanium structure.
  - It is also why sustained Mach 3 conflicts with polymer-matrix composites, radar-absorbing materials (RAM) and IR stealth: hot skin radiates in the IR bands that IRST sensors use.
  - This is an inference; material temperature limits were not sourced in this session.
- The US pattern shows speed being deliberately traded for survivability by signature. Interceptor-era Mach 3 was abandoned on cost and threat grounds (ICBMs, SAMs). The ATF fixed supercruise at about Mach 1.5–1.8 and took stealth over top speed, limited by available engine technology. A Mach 3 stealth fighter is therefore a deliberate departure from US doctrine. The design study should state up front what threat or mission justifies Mach 3: a time-critical intercept, or kinematic advantage for missiles.
- An F-22-style fixed caret inlet and a composite-heavy skin are optimized for about Mach 1.5–2. Mach 3 implies variable-geometry inlets like the SR-71's translating spike, and those are hard to make low-observable. The engineering tension here is inferred, not sourced.

## 6. Lessons learned: cost growth and schedule slips (GAO), LO maintenance burden, software complexity, sustainment cost per flight hour

### Takeaway
GAO data show three recurring problems:
- **Development cost growth leading to buy cuts.** F-22 development costs grew 127% to $28.7 billion by 2004, and the planned buy fell from 750 to 187 operational aircraft.
- **Sustainment far larger than acquisition.** F-35 sustainment is estimated at ≥$1.58 trillion over a 77-year life cycle, bringing the program total above $2 trillion.
- **Flying-hour costs that stay high.** The F-22 costs about $80,000–85,000 per flight hour. The F-35 program office claims costs are falling, but planned annual hours per F-35A dropped from 230 to 187.

Software growth and late deliveries are now the main F-35 schedule drivers.

### Inferences
- The F-22 shows the cost spiral: development cost growth leads to a smaller buy, which raises program unit cost (about $143 million flyaway against about $370 million program unit cost), which shrinks the fleet and raises per-aircraft support cost. A design that pushes several frontiers at once (Mach 3, broadband LO, a new engine cycle) would sit at the far end of this risk curve.
- LO upkeep cost:
  - F-22 cost per flight hour (about $85,000) is more than 2× the F-35's (about $34,000 claimed by the JPO).
  - The F-35 was designed for more durable, maintainable LO, which suggests maintainability-oriented LO design pays off in operation.
  - Assumption to verify: at Mach 3, sustained heating and rain/particle erosion would likely make LO coating durability worse.
- Software (about 24 million lines total for the F-35) and engine maturity (the NGAP delay) are the long-pole items in current US programs, ahead of the airframe. A Mach 3 stealth study should budget explicitly for propulsion and integration risk.
- Sustainment (≥$1.58 trillion) is larger than acquisition for the F-35. Life-cycle cost (LO repair hours, engine hot-section life at Mach 3 temperatures) should be a design objective from the concept stage.
